AMERICAN HISTORY THROUGH TIME — All 486 cards (one line each)

=== Native Nations and Exploration  (31 cards) ===
  Ice Age — The First Americans Arrive: The first people reached North America by crossing a land bridge called Beringia.
  Ice Age — The Bering Land Bridge: The land bridge connected Asia to modern-day Alaska during the Ice Age.
  Ancient times — Nomadic Hunters: These early people were nomads who moved constantly to hunt animals.
  Over millennia — Filling Two Continents: Over many generations, these groups spread south all the way to South America.
  Pre-1492 — Hundreds of Different Cultures: Diverse civilizations developed, each adapting uniquely to its environment.
  c. 750–1300 — Ancestral Puebloans: The Ancestral Puebloans built remarkable cliff dwellings in the Southwest.
  c. 1050–1350 — The Mississippians & Cahokia: The Mississippians built giant earthen mounds, like the great city of Cahokia.
  Pre-1492 — The Iroquois Confederacy: The Iroquois formed a powerful alliance that united five nations in peace.
  Pre-1492 — The Three Sisters: Native farmers grew corn, beans, and squash together as “The Three Sisters.”
  Pre-1492 — The Inuit of the Arctic: The Inuit adapted to the freezing temperatures of the far northern Arctic.
  Pre-1492 — The Plains Nations & the Bison: Plains tribes like the Sioux relied heavily on the American bison.
  Pre-1492 — Oral Tradition: Native Americans used storytelling to pass down history, laws, and beliefs.
★ c. 1000 — Leif Erikson Reaches America: Around the year 1000, the Norse explorer Leif Erikson reached North America.
  c. 1000 — The Viking Settlement of Vinland: The Vikings called their short-lived North American settlement “Vinland.”
★ 1492 — Columbus Sails West: Sailing for Spain, Christopher Columbus crossed the Atlantic in 1492 looking for Asia.
  1492 — Mistaken for the “Indies”: Columbus landed in the Caribbean but thought he had reached the Indies near Asia.
★ After 1492 — The Columbian Exchange: A massive swap of plants, animals, people, and diseases flowed between the worlds.
  After 1492 — New Foods for the Old World: The Americas gave the world potatoes, tomatoes, corn, and chocolate.
  After 1492 — New Animals for the Americas: The Americas received horses, cattle, wheat, and honeybees from Europe.
  After 1492 — Disease Devastates Native Peoples: Europeans unknowingly brought diseases that devastated Native populations.
  1500s — The Spanish Conquistadors: Spanish explorers called conquistadors arrived seeking gold and riches.
  1513 — Ponce de León in Florida: Juan Ponce de León explored Florida in 1513, searching for gold and youth.
  1565 — St. Augustine Founded: Spain founded St. Augustine in Florida in 1565.
  1565 → today — America’s Oldest City: St. Augustine is the oldest continuously inhabited European-founded city in the U.S.
  1530s — French Exploration of Canada: French explorer Jacques Cartier explored the St. Lawrence River area.
  1600s — The French Fur Trade: The French focused heavily on the highly lucrative fur trade.
  1609 — Henry Hudson Explores: English explorer Henry Hudson explored the river and bay now named for him.
  1500s — England Wants Colonies: England wanted colonies to match the growing wealth and power of Spain.
  1585 — The Roanoke Colony: Sir Walter Raleigh sponsored England’s first attempt to colonize Roanoke Island.
★ 1590 — The Lost Colony: The Roanoke colony completely vanished by 1590, leaving a lasting mystery.
  1590 — The “Croatoan” Clue: The only clue left at Roanoke was the word “Croatoan” carved into a post.


=== The Thirteen Colonies  (54 cards) ===
★ 1607 — Jamestown Is Founded: English settlers built Jamestown in Virginia — the first permanent English colony in America.
  1607 — The First Permanent English Colony: Jamestown became the very first permanent English settlement in North America.
  1609–1610 — The "Starving Time": The early Jamestown settlers suffered heavily from hunger, disease, and cold.
  1608 — Captain John Smith Takes Charge: Captain John Smith took leadership of Jamestown to save it from collapse.
  1608 — "He Who Will Not Work…": Smith’s famous rule for the settlers was: "He who will not work shall not eat."
  c. 1607 — Pocahontas: Pocahontas was the daughter of Powhatan, a powerful local Native chief.
  1614–1622 — A Fragile Peace: Pocahontas helped keep peace between her tribe and the English settlers.
  1612 onward — Tobacco Saves Virginia: Tobacco became Virginia’s cash crop, making the colony highly profitable.
  1619 — First Enslaved Africans: In 1619, the first enslaved Africans were brought to Virginia.
★ 1619 — The House of Burgesses: In 1619, the House of Burgesses met, starting representative government in America.
★ 1620 — The Pilgrims and the Mayflower: The Pilgrims sailed to America in 1620 on a ship called the Mayflower.
  1620 — Who Were the Pilgrims?: The Pilgrims were Separatists who wanted total freedom from the Church of England.
  1620 — The Mayflower Compact: They signed the Mayflower Compact to create fair laws for their new colony.
  1620 — Landing at Plymouth: The Pilgrims landed at Plymouth Rock in modern-day Massachusetts.
  1621 — Squanto Helps the Pilgrims: Squanto, a Patuxet Native American, taught the Pilgrims how to plant corn using fish.
  1621 — The First Thanksgiving: In autumn 1621, the Pilgrims and Wampanoag celebrated the first Thanksgiving.
  1630 — The Puritans Arrive: The Puritans arrived in 1630, founding the Massachusetts Bay Colony.
  1630s — Why "Puritans"?: The Puritans wanted to reform, or "purify," the Church of England.
  1600s — Boston, a Trading Hub: Boston quickly grew into a major shipping and trading hub for New England.
  1607–1733 — Three Regions of Colonies: The British colonies were divided into three regions: New England, Middle, and Southern.
  1600s — The New England Colonies: New England colonies included Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, and New Hampshire.
  1600s — Rocky Soil, Long Winters: New England had rocky soil, long winters, and plenty of thick forests.
  1600s — Fishing, Ships, and Trade: The New England economy relied on shipbuilding, fishing, and ocean trade.
  1636 — Roger Williams & Rhode Island: Roger Williams founded Rhode Island to offer true religious freedom.
  1638 — Anne Hutchinson: Anne Hutchinson was banished from Massachusetts for preaching her own religious ideas.
  1600s — The Middle Colonies: The Middle Colonies included New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Delaware.
  1600s — The "Breadbasket": The Middle Colonies were nicknamed the "Breadbasket" because they grew so much wheat.
  1624 — New Netherland (Dutch New York): New York was originally a Dutch colony called New Netherland.
  1664 — England Takes New York: The English took over New Netherland in 1664 without firing a single shot.
  1681 — William Penn & Pennsylvania: William Penn founded Pennsylvania as a safe refuge for Quakers.
  1682 — Philadelphia: Philadelphia, Pennsylvania’s capital, means "The City of Brotherly Love."
  1600s — The Southern Colonies: The Southern Colonies were Maryland, Virginia, the Carolinas, and Georgia.
  1600s–1700s — Plantations: The Southern economy relied on large farms called plantations.
  1600s–1700s — Cash Crops: Southern plantations grew cash crops like tobacco, rice, and indigo dye.
  1600s–1700s — Forced Labor: Plantations depended heavily on the forced labor of enslaved people.
  1634 — Maryland, a Catholic Haven: Maryland was founded by Lord Baltimore as a safe haven for Catholics.
  1733 — Georgia, a Fresh Start: James Oglethorpe founded Georgia as a colony for debtors to start over.
  1730s — Georgia as a Buffer: Georgia also served as a military buffer against Spanish-owned Florida.
  1700s — Benjamin Franklin, Inventor: Benjamin Franklin was an inventor who created the lightning rod and bifocals.
  1732 — Poor Richard’s Almanack: Franklin published Poor Richard’s Almanack, filled with wise, witty sayings.
  1730s–1740s — The Great Awakening: The Great Awakening was a major religious revival movement in the 1730s and 1740s.
  1700s — Everyday Colonial Life: Most colonists lived on small family farms and made their own clothes.
  1700s — Learning with Hornbooks: Hornbooks — wooden paddles with lesson sheets — were used to teach reading.
  1700s — Colonial Children’s Games: Colonial children played games like marbles, hopscotch, and rolling hoops.
★ 1754–1763 — The French and Indian War: The French and Indian War broke out between Britain and France in 1754.
  1750s — Fight for the Ohio Valley: Both sides fought fiercely for control of the fertile Ohio River Valley.
  1750s — Native Nations Choose Sides: Many Native American tribes allied with the French against the British.
  1754 — Young George Washington: A young George Washington fought as a British military officer during this war.
  1763 — Britain Wins the War: Great Britain won the French and Indian War in 1763.
  1763 — The Treaty of Paris (1763): The Treaty of Paris of 1763 officially ended the war.
  1763 — France Loses North America: France lost almost all of its North American territory to Great Britain.
  1763 — A Mountain of Debt: The war left Great Britain with a massive national debt.
  1763 — The Proclamation of 1763: King George III issued the Proclamation of 1763 to stop westward settlement.
  1763 — Closing the Frontier: The Proclamation forbade colonists from settling west of the Appalachian Mountains.


=== The American Revolution  (49 cards) ===
  1763 — Britain Decides to Tax: Parliament decided to tax the colonists to pay off the massive war debt.
  1764 — The Sugar Act: The Sugar Act of 1764 placed taxes on sugar and molasses imports.
  1765 — The Stamp Act: The Stamp Act of 1765 taxed paper items like newspapers and playing cards.
  1765 — "No Taxation Without Representation": Colonists protested using the slogan: "No taxation without representation!"
  1765 — The Quartering Act: The Quartering Act forced colonists to provide housing for British soldiers.
  1765 — The Sons of Liberty: The Sons of Liberty formed as a secret group to protest British taxes.
  1767 — The Townshend Acts: The Townshend Acts of 1767 taxed everyday imports like glass, lead, paint, and tea.
  1768 — Colonists Boycott British Goods: Colonists boycotted British goods, refusing to buy them to show resistance.
  1770 — The Boston Massacre: The Boston Massacre happened in 1770 when British soldiers fired into an angry crowd.
  1770 — Crispus Attucks: Crispus Attucks, an African-Native American man, was the first killed at Boston.
  1773 — The Tax on Tea: Parliament repealed most taxes but kept the tax on tea to prove its authority.
★ 1773 — The Boston Tea Party: The Boston Tea Party took place on December 16, 1773.
  1773 — Disguised as Mohawks: The Sons of Liberty disguised themselves as Mohawk Indians and boarded tea ships.
  1773 — 342 Chests of Tea: They dumped 342 chests of British tea directly into Boston Harbor.
  1774 — The Coercive (Intolerable) Acts: Britain passed the Coercive Acts, which colonists called the Intolerable Acts.
  1774 — Boston Harbor Closed: The Intolerable Acts closed Boston Harbor and banned local town meetings.
  1774 — The First Continental Congress: The First Continental Congress met in Philadelphia in 1774 to protest.
  1774–1775 — The Minutemen: Colonists formed volunteer citizen militias known proudly as Minutemen.
  1775 — Ready in a Minute: Minutemen earned their name because they could be ready to fight in a minute.
★ 1775 — Paul Revere’s Midnight Ride: Paul Revere made his famous midnight ride to warn that British troops were coming.
  1775 — The Revolutionary War Begins: The Revolutionary War began on April 19, 1775, in Massachusetts.
★ 1775 — Lexington and Concord: The battles of Lexington and Concord marked the start of the war.
  1775 — "The Shot Heard ’Round the World": The opening shot at Lexington is called "the shot heard ’round the world."
  1775 — The Second Continental Congress: The Second Continental Congress created the official Continental Army.
  1775 — Washington Takes Command: George Washington was chosen as the Commander-in-Chief of the army.
  1775 — The Battle of Bunker Hill: The Battle of Bunker Hill showed the British that Americans would fight hard.
  1776 — Thomas Paine’s "Common Sense": Thomas Paine wrote a pamphlet called Common Sense urging independence.
  1776 — Jefferson Writes the Draft: Thomas Jefferson wrote the primary draft of the Declaration of Independence.
  1776 — "Life, Liberty, and Happiness": The Declaration says all people have rights to "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness."
★ 1776 — The Declaration of Independence: The Continental Congress officially adopted the Declaration on July 4, 1776.
  1776 — Independence Day: July 4 is celebrated every year as America’s Independence Day.
  1776 — Patriots: People who supported independence were called Patriots or Whigs.
  1776 — Loyalists: People who wanted to stay loyal to the King were called Loyalists or Tories.
  1776 — Crossing the Delaware: On Christmas night 1776, Washington crossed the icy Delaware River.
  1776 — Victory at Trenton: Washington led a surprise attack on Hessian forces in Trenton, New Jersey.
  1777 — The Turning Point: Saratoga: The American victory at the Battle of Saratoga was the major turning point.
  1778 — France Joins the Fight: Saratoga convinced France to sign an alliance and help the American cause.
  1777–1778 — Winter at Valley Forge: The Continental Army spent a brutal, freezing winter at Valley Forge.
  1778 — Baron von Steuben: Baron von Steuben trained the soldiers at Valley Forge into a professional army.
  1777 — The Marquis de Lafayette: Marquis de Lafayette was a young French noble who volunteered to help Washington.
  1779 — John Paul Jones: John Paul Jones was a naval hero who declared, "I have not yet begun to fight!"
  1780 — Benedict Arnold’s Betrayal: Benedict Arnold was an American general who infamously switched sides to the British.
  1780–1781 — General Cornwallis: General Cornwallis led the British army in the Southern colonies.
  1781 — The Battle of Yorktown: The final major battle of the war was the Battle of Yorktown in 1781.
  1781 — Cornwallis Surrenders: Surrounded by French ships and American troops, Cornwallis surrendered at Yorktown.
  1783 — The Treaty of Paris (1783): The Treaty of Paris of 1783 officially recognized American independence.
  1781 — The Articles of Confederation: The Articles of Confederation served as the first, very weak U.S. government.
  1781–1787 — A Government Too Weak: Under the Articles, the federal government could not collect taxes or print money.
  1786 — Shays’ Rebellion: Shays’ Rebellion in 1786 showed that the weak government could not maintain order.


=== The Young U.S.A.  (44 cards) ===
  1787 — The Constitutional Convention: Constitutional Convention delegates met in Philadelphia in 1787 to fix the government.
★ 1787 — A Brand-New Constitution: Instead of fixing the Articles, they wrote an entirely new U.S. Constitution.
  1787 — "Father of the Constitution": James Madison is called the "Father of the Constitution" for his major role.
  1787 — Three Branches: The Constitution divided the government into three separate branches.
  1787 — The Legislative Branch: The Legislative Branch (Congress) makes the nation’s laws.
  1787 — The Executive Branch: The Executive Branch (the President) enforces the nation’s laws.
  1787 — The Judicial Branch: The Judicial Branch (the Supreme Court) interprets the nation’s laws.
  1787 — Checks and Balances: A system of checks and balances prevents any single branch from becoming too powerful.
  1787 — The Great Compromise: The Great Compromise created a two-house Congress: the Senate and House of Representatives.
  1787–1788 — Federalists vs. Anti-Federalists: Federalists supported the Constitution; Anti-Federalists worried it gave government too much power.
★ 1791 — The Bill of Rights: To protect citizen rights, the Bill of Rights was added to the Constitution.
  1791 — The First Ten Amendments: The Bill of Rights consists of the first ten amendments to the Constitution.
  1791 — The First Amendment: The First Amendment protects freedom of speech, religion, press, and assembly.
★ 1789 — Washington, First President: George Washington was unanimously elected as the first President of the United States.
  1789 — The First Inauguration: Washington took the oath of office in New York City, the temporary capital.
  1797 — Stepping Down After Two Terms: Washington chose to step down after two terms, setting an important future precedent.
  1797 — John Adams, Second President: John Adams was elected as the second President of the United States.
  1800 — The First White House Resident: Adams was the very first president to live in the newly built White House.
  1801 — Thomas Jefferson, Third President: Thomas Jefferson was elected as the third President of the United States.
★ 1803 — The Louisiana Purchase: In 1803, Jefferson purchased the Louisiana Territory from France for $15 million.
  1803 — Doubling the Nation: The Louisiana Purchase instantly doubled the geographic size of the nation.
  1804 — Lewis and Clark: Meriwether Lewis and William Clark were hired to explore the new western land.
  1804 — The Corps of Discovery: Their expedition team was officially called the Corps of Discovery.
  1805 — Sacagawea: A Shoshone woman named Sacagawea served as their vital guide and translator.
  1805 — Reaching the Pacific: The expedition reached the Pacific Ocean in 1805 and mapped the wilderness.
  1812–1815 — The War of 1812: The War of 1812 broke out between the United States and Great Britain.
  1812 — Impressment at Sea: A major cause of the war was the British impressment (kidnapping) of American sailors.
  1814 — Washington Burns: During the war, British troops marched into Washington, D.C., and burned the White House.
  1814 — Dolley Madison’s Rescue: First Lady Dolley Madison saved a famous portrait of George Washington from the fire.
  1814 — Francis Scott Key’s Poem: Francis Scott Key wrote a poem during the bombardment of Fort McHenry.
  1814 — "The Star-Spangled Banner": This famous poem later became "The Star-Spangled Banner," America’s national anthem.
  1815 — The Battle of New Orleans: General Andrew Jackson became a national hero at the Battle of New Orleans.
  1815 — The Treaty of Ghent: The Treaty of Ghent ended the War of 1812, restoring peace.
  early 1800s — The Industrial Revolution: The Industrial Revolution began changing how goods were made, from hand tools to machines.
  1793 — Eli Whitney’s Cotton Gin: Eli Whitney invented the cotton gin in 1793, speeding up cotton seed removal.
  early 1800s — A Tragic Side Effect: The cotton gin unintentionally caused a massive increase in the demand for enslaved labor.
  1825 — The Erie Canal: The Erie Canal opened in 1825, connecting the Hudson River to the Great Lakes.
  1825 — New York Rises: The canal made New York City the nation’s premier center of commerce.
  1823 — The Monroe Doctrine: The Monroe Doctrine of 1823 warned European nations to stay out of the Americas.
  1828 — Andrew Jackson Elected: Andrew Jackson was elected as the seventh U.S. President in 1828.
  1830 — The Indian Removal Act: Jackson signed the devastating Indian Removal Act into law in 1830.
  1830s — Forced From Their Lands: Native American tribes were forced to leave their ancestral lands in the Southeast.
  1838 — The Long March West: They were forced to march hundreds of miles to Indian Territory in modern Oklahoma.
  1838 — The Trail of Tears: This forced march is known as the Trail of Tears because thousands died.


=== The Civil War & Westward Expansion  (72 cards) ===
  1840s — Manifest Destiny: "Manifest Destiny" was the widespread belief that America should expand coast to coast.
  1836 — Texas Fights for Independence: Texas fought for its independence from Mexico in the year 1836.
  1836 — The Battle of the Alamo: The Battle of the Alamo was a famous siege where Texan defenders were defeated.
  1836 — "Remember the Alamo!": "Remember the Alamo!" became the rallying cry for Texan independence.
  1836–1845 — The Republic of Texas: Texas existed as an independent republic before joining the United States in 1845.
  1846 — The Mexican-American War: The Mexican-American War began in 1846 over a border dispute in Texas.
  1848 — A Vast New Territory: The U.S. won the war, gaining land that became California, Utah, Nevada, and Arizona.
  1840s–1860s — The Oregon Trail: The Oregon Trail was a 2,000-mile route used by thousands of pioneers moving west.
  1840s–1860s — Wagon Trains: Pioneers traveled the long trails together in large groups called wagon trains.
  1848 — Gold at Sutter’s Mill: Gold was discovered at Sutter’s Mill in California in 1848.
★ 1849 — The Forty-Niners: In 1849, over 80,000 gold-seekers rushed to California; they were called "49ers."
  1850 — California Becomes a State: The California Gold Rush led to rapid population growth and statehood by 1850.
  1830s–1860s — The Steam Locomotive: Steam locomotives began replacing horses, revolutionizing long-distance travel.
  1844 — Morse and the Telegraph: Samuel Morse invented the telegraph and Morse code, making instant communication possible.
  1830s–1860s — The Abolitionist Movement: The abolitionist movement grew quickly, dedicated to ending slavery in America.
  1800s — The Underground Railroad: The Underground Railroad was a secret network helping enslaved people escape north.
  1849+ — Harriet Tubman: Harriet Tubman escaped slavery in 1849 and risked her life to guide others to freedom.
  1850s — "Moses" of Her People: Tubman was proudly nicknamed "Moses" for leading her people to freedom.
  1840s+ — Frederick Douglass: Frederick Douglass escaped slavery and became a powerful writer, orator, and activist.
  1852 — Uncle Tom’s Cabin: Harriet Beecher Stowe wrote Uncle Tom’s Cabin, a book that exposed the horrors of slavery.
  1848 — The Seneca Falls Convention: The Seneca Falls Convention of 1848 was the first women’s rights convention in America.
  1848 — The Declaration of Sentiments: Elizabeth Cady Stanton wrote the Declaration of Sentiments demanding women’s right to vote.
  1820 — The Missouri Compromise: The Missouri Compromise of 1820 tried to balance free and slave states.
  1850 — The Compromise of 1850: The Compromise of 1850 included the strict Fugitive Slave Act.
  1850 — The Fugitive Slave Act: The Fugitive Slave Act required citizens to help capture escaped enslaved people.
  1850s — "Bleeding Kansas": "Bleeding Kansas" was a period of violent conflict over whether Kansas would allow slavery.
  1857 — The Dred Scott Decision: The Supreme Court’s Dred Scott decision ruled that enslaved people were not citizens.
  1860 — Lincoln Is Elected: Abraham Lincoln was elected as the 16th President of the United States in 1860.
  1860 — South Carolina Secedes: South Carolina became the very first state to secede (leave) from the Union.
  1861 — The Confederacy Forms: Eleven Southern states seceded to form the Confederate States of America.
  1861 — Jefferson Davis: Jefferson Davis was chosen as the President of the Confederacy.
★ 1861 — The Civil War Begins: The Civil War began on April 12, 1861, at Fort Sumter, South Carolina.
  1861 — Fort Sumter Falls: Confederate forces fired on the federal fort, forcing its surrender.
  1861 — The Union (the North): The North was called the Union, and their soldiers wore blue uniforms.
  1861 — The Confederacy (the South): The South was called the Confederacy, and their soldiers wore gray uniforms.
  1861 — The Union’s Strengths: The Union had more factories, railroads, and a much larger population.
  1861 — The Confederacy’s Strengths: The Confederacy had excellent military generals and fought on familiar home territory.
  1861 — The First Battle of Bull Run: The First Battle of Bull Run was the first major land battle of the war.
  1861 — "Stonewall" Jackson: Confederate General Thomas Jackson earned his famous nickname "Stonewall" at Bull Run.
  1862 — General Robert E. Lee: General Robert E. Lee was chosen to command the Confederate Army of Northern Virginia.
  1862 — The Battle of Antietam: The Battle of Antietam in 1862 was the single bloodiest day in American military history.
★ 1863 — The Emancipation Proclamation: President Lincoln issued the historic Emancipation Proclamation on January 1, 1863.
  1863 — Declaring Freedom: The Proclamation declared that all enslaved people in Confederate-held lands were free.
  1863 — Black Soldiers Join the Fight: It also allowed African American men to officially enlist in the Union Army.
  1863 — The 54th Massachusetts: The 54th Massachusetts Infantry was one of the most famous all-Black Union regiments.
  1860s — Clara Barton: Clara Barton worked as a frontline nurse and later founded the American Red Cross.
★ 1863 — The Battle of Gettysburg: The Battle of Gettysburg in July 1863 was the ultimate turning point of the war.
  1863 — Lee’s Invasion Ends: The Union victory at Gettysburg ended General Robert E. Lee’s invasion of the North.
  1863 — The Fall of Vicksburg: General Ulysses S. Grant captured Vicksburg, giving the Union control of the Mississippi River.
  1863 — The Gettysburg Address: Lincoln delivered the timeless Gettysburg Address to dedicate a national cemetery.
  1863 — "Four Score and Seven…": The short speech begins with the famous words: "Four score and seven years ago..."
  1864 — Sherman’s March to the Sea: Union General William T. Sherman led a destructive "March to the Sea" through Georgia.
  1865 — Lee Surrenders: General Robert E. Lee surrendered to General Ulysses S. Grant on April 9, 1865.
  1865 — Appomattox Court House: The surrender took place at Appomattox Court House in Virginia, ending the war.
  1865 — Lincoln Is Assassinated: On April 14, 1865, President Lincoln was assassinated by John Wilkes Booth.
  1865 — Ford’s Theatre: Lincoln was shot while watching a play at Ford’s Theatre in Washington, D.C.
  1865–1877 — Reconstruction: Reconstruction was the era dedicated to rebuilding the South and reuniting the country.
  1865 — The 13th Amendment: The 13th Amendment was ratified, completely abolishing slavery in the United States.
  1868 — The 14th Amendment: The 14th Amendment granted full citizenship to all people born in the United States.
  1870 — The 15th Amendment: The 15th Amendment protected the right to vote regardless of race or color.
  1865 — The Freedmen’s Bureau: The Freedmen’s Bureau was established to help former slaves find jobs and get an education.
  1868 — President Andrew Johnson: Andrew Johnson became president after Lincoln’s death and faced impeachment by Congress.
  1870s — Sharecropping: Sharecropping became common in the South, keeping many freed people trapped in deep debt.
  1870s+ — Jim Crow Laws: "Jim Crow" laws were passed across the South to enforce racial segregation.
  1870s+ — Segregation: Segregation meant separating Black and white Americans in public spaces.
  1869 — The Transcontinental Railroad: The Transcontinental Railroad was completed in 1869 at Promontory Summit, Utah.
  1869 — The Golden Spike: A golden spike was driven into the ground to connect the Atlantic and Pacific rails.
  1860s — The Railroad Workers: Thousands of Chinese and Irish immigrants built the dangerous Transcontinental Railroad.
  1862 — The Homestead Act: The Homestead Act of 1862 offered 160 acres of free western land to settlers.
  1862 — Earning the Land: Settlers had to live on the land and farm it for five years to own it.
  1860s+ — The Buffalo Soldiers: Buffalo Soldiers were elite regiments of African American cavalrymen serving out west.
  1877 — Reconstruction Ends: Reconstruction officially ended in 1877 when federal troops left the South.


=== The Industrial Era & World War I  (50 cards) ===
★ 1870s–1900 — The Gilded Age: The Gilded Age was a time of massive economic growth but severe hidden poverty.
  1876 — The Telephone: Alexander Graham Bell invented the telephone in 1876, changing communication forever.
  1879 — Edison’s Lightbulb: Thomas Edison perfected the incandescent electric lightbulb at his Menlo Park lab.
  1880s+ — The Wizard of Menlo Park: Edison held over 1,000 patents, earning him the nickname "The Wizard of Menlo Park."
  late 1800s — Captains of Industry: Corporations grew rapidly, led by incredibly wealthy "captains of industry."
  late 1800s — Andrew Carnegie: Andrew Carnegie dominated the steel industry and later gave away his fortune to libraries.
  late 1800s — John D. Rockefeller: John D. Rockefeller founded Standard Oil, becoming America’s very first billionaire.
★ 1892 — Ellis Island Opens: Ellis Island opened in New York Harbor in 1892 as an immigration processing station.
  1892–1924 — A Nation of Immigrants: More than 12 million immigrants entered the United States through Ellis Island.
  1910 — Angel Island: Angel Island opened in San Francisco in 1910, processing mostly Asian immigrants.
  late 1800s — Urbanization: Cities grew rapidly, a trend known as urbanization.
  late 1800s — Tenement Life: Many poor immigrant families lived in crowded, unsafe apartment buildings called tenements.
  late 1800s — Labor Unions: Labor unions formed to fight for better pay, shorter hours, and safer working conditions.
  1889 — Jane Addams and Hull House: Jane Addams founded Hull House in Chicago to help poor immigrants adjust to city life.
  1898 — The Spanish-American War: The Spanish-American War broke out in 1898 after the USS Maine exploded in Cuba.
  1898 — The Rough Riders: Theodore Roosevelt led a famous volunteer cavalry unit called the "Rough Riders."
  1898 — America Gains Territory: The U.S. won the war, gaining control of Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines.
  1890s–1920s — The Progressive Era: The Progressive Era was a period focused on fixing social and political problems.
  early 1900s — The Muckrakers: "Muckrakers" were journalists who exposed corruption and bad conditions in society.
  1906 — The Jungle: Upton Sinclair wrote The Jungle, exposing unsanitary conditions in the meatpacking industry.
  1906 — The Pure Food and Drug Act: Sinclair’s book led directly to the passage of the Pure Food and Drug Act.
  1901 — President Theodore Roosevelt: Theodore Roosevelt became president in 1901 after William McKinley’s assassination.
  1900s — Roosevelt the Conservationist: Roosevelt was a passionate conservationist who created the U.S. Forest Service.
  1900s — Protecting the Land: Roosevelt established 5 national parks and protected 150 national forests.
★ 1903 — The Wright Brothers: Orville and Wilbur Wright made the first successful controlled airplane flight in 1903.
  1903 — Kitty Hawk: The historic Wright brothers’ flight took place at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina.
  1908 — The Model T: Henry Ford introduced the Model T automobile in 1908, making cars affordable.
  1913 — The Assembly Line: Ford revolutionized manufacturing by using the moving assembly line.
  1914 — The Panama Canal: The United States built the Panama Canal, opening a vital shipping shortcut in 1914.
  1914 — World War I Begins: World War I began in Europe in 1914 between the Allied and Central Powers.
  1914–1917 — Trying to Stay Out: President Woodrow Wilson tried hard to keep the United States out of the conflict.
  1915–1917 — German U-Boats: Germany used submarines called U-boats to sink American merchant ships.
  1915 — The Lusitania: The sinking of the British passenger liner Lusitania in 1915 killed 128 Americans.
  1917 — The Zimmerman Telegram: The Zimmerman Telegram revealed Germany’s secret plan to get Mexico to attack the U.S.
